# EcoSort and Universal Human Values

## Assigned problem

**Waste Management — Group 2:** “Garbage dumped in open areas without segregation or collection systems (Vellangallur).”

The accompanying scenario describes mixed household garbage accumulating in public plots and roadsides, with no clear coordination for collection and appropriate destinations. We treat this as the assigned college scenario, not independent evidence about current local municipal services.

## Our response

EcoSort addresses the missing **system of cooperation**, not just the visible dumping symptom:

1. Households learn category-specific segregation and explicitly confirm separation.
2. Residents submit a pickup request with usable location and quantity information.
3. An administrator reviews the request and assigns an authorized collector.
4. The assigned collector starts the job and explicitly records physical collection.
5. A timestamp, optional evidence, and intended destination or actual handover destination are recorded.
6. The resident sees the completed journey, while administrators see changing operational totals.

Existing public dumping sites use a distinct reporting journey. Reporting is important, but it is secondary to preventing unsegregated waste from reaching open spaces.

## Responsibility

Household segregation is a conscious action, not an automatic claim by the app. Residents must confirm it. Collectors must confirm physical collection rather than arbitrarily changing a status. Reasons are required for closure, and status history records each step. An intended destination is clearly distinguished from actual handover.

Responsibility also means honesty: collection is not presented as verified recycling, all demo data is fictional, and no imaginary AI or municipal partnership is claimed.

## Cooperation

Residents, collectors and administrators have different responsibilities within one shared workflow. Assignment makes the next responsible person visible. Scoped access gives collectors their jobs without exposing every household's information. Administrators coordinate available people and review evidence rather than assuming reports resolve themselves.

Cooperation must extend outside the screen: real workers, safe transport, suitable facilities and authorized hazardous handling need human agreements.

## Participation

Residents can act through segregation, pickup requests, public-site reports and progress tracking. Mobile-first navigation and simple collector tasks reduce avoidable technical barriers. GPS is optional: addresses, landmarks, manual coordinates and map selection remain available if permission is denied.

The classroom demo makes all roles accessible for learning, but connected registration never lets a public user choose administrative powers.

## Harmony with nature

Keeping materials separate supports safer handling and reduces contamination. The guide encourages reduction and reuse, avoids claiming all dry waste is recyclable, and treats batteries, chemicals and e-waste as requiring appropriate authorized handling. Traceable destination records encourage follow-through without inventing environmental outcomes.

Environmental harmony is demonstrated through careful practices and accountable coordination, not inflated impact statistics.

## What technology can and cannot do

EcoSort can organize information, connect responsibilities and make recorded action visible. It cannot create collection services, train workers, certify a treatment facility, establish municipal partnerships or independently prove recycling occurred. Those are operational prerequisites and future verification work.

The working demo models responsible participation. A real deployment requires local consultation, safe material acceptance rules, trained staff, privacy/retention arrangements and verified destination relationships. Human care remains the foundation; technology is a supporting tool.
